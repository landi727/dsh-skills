# Explain Concepts

## Purpose

Enable the reader to understand what a concept does, how it works, where its boundary lies, and how to judge a real example.

## Control depth

- Answer simple questions in a few direct sentences.
- Use the full method only for complex, ambiguous, or easily confused concepts.
- Include only dimensions that change understanding or judgment.
- State the conclusion before background.

## Method

### 1. Define its job

Identify the field, level, and practical problem the concept addresses. Define it through this relation:

> It takes **what input or situation**, applies **what distinctive action or relationship**, and produces or determines **what result**.

Use concrete actors, objects, actions, and consequences. If the term has several established meanings, select the meaning relevant here and mention only ambiguity that could change the answer.

### 2. Explain its mechanism

Trace the necessary chain:

> starting condition → stable rule or action → resulting change → observable result

When the concept belongs to a process, state what information changes at this step. Do not force a hierarchy when the actual relationship is overlapping, conditional, or disputed.

### 3. Clarify its boundary

Compare only the nearest concepts that users may confuse with it. Keep the comparison on shared dimensions:

- job performed;
- object acted on;
- output produced;
- time or scope, when relevant.

Explain the practical consequence of substituting one concept for another. Use direct affirmative contrasts.

### 4. Make judgment possible

When asked what makes the concept “good,” derive standards from the job it must perform. Convert every standard into an observable question or consequence. Choose only the tests that help:

- **Qualification:** What evidence must exist for the label to apply?
- **Removal:** What weakens or disappears when the defining mechanism is removed?
- **Substitution:** Can a nearby concept replace it without changing the result?
- **Generation:** For a generative concept, what stays fixed, what may vary, and can it produce genuinely different outputs?

Use a valid and an invalid case only when they clarify the boundary. Explain why each passes or fails.

## Writing constraints

- Add historical, social, technological, media, or audience context only when it changes the concept's role, conditions, or quality standard; show that causal link.
- Explain abstract language through concrete relationships, actions, or results.
- Do not replace a definition with praise words such as “simple,” “unique,” “relevant,” or “powerful.”
- Keep terminology stable and classifications on one dimension per level.
- Separate established meaning, reasoned interpretation, and recommendation when they coexist.
- State genuine uncertainty or disagreement and avoid repeated conclusions.

## Final check

Verify that the answer:

1. states what the concept actually does;
2. contains a traceable mechanism;
3. gives a specific boundary when needed;
4. turns quality claims into usable tests;
5. remains proportionate to the question.
