# Visual Analysis Dimensions

Use all dimensions, but vary depth according to the image. For every dimension, move from visible evidence to operating principle to stylistic effect. Do not turn the questions below into mechanical questionnaire output.

## 1. Overall visual system

Establish the whole before discussing parts:

- Determine whether the image is organized primarily through drawing, flat graphic shapes, tonal painting, photography, collage, typography, or a hybrid system.
- Identify the first visual anchors and the dominant visual order.
- Describe the broad relationship among image density, negative space, scale, color, and depth.
- State what differentiates the image from an ordinary example with similar subject matter.
- Keep any period, movement, or medium attribution separate and tentative.

## 2. Format, frame, and boundary

Analyze:

- orientation and aspect relationship;
- visible borders, margins, paper fields, bleed, or internal frames;
- whether major forms touch, cross, or are cut by the frame;
- whether cropping suggests scale, proximity, tension, continuation, or accidental loss;
- whether the boundary behaves as a neutral container or an active compositional device.

## 3. Composition and hierarchy

Analyze:

- primary, secondary, and supporting visual masses;
- location and stability of the visual center of gravity;
- symmetric, asymmetric, centralized, dispersed, diagonal, radial, or grid organization;
- relative areas of large, medium, and small forms;
- distribution of negative space and visual congestion;
- eye path and the devices that redirect attention;
- relationship between detailed zones and quiet zones;
- whether the composition feels stable, tense, spacious, compressed, staged, or immersive, and why.

## 4. Space and perspective

Analyze:

- presence or absence of a horizon, vanishing point, foreshortening, or consistent projection;
- depth cues from overlap, vertical placement, scale change, focus, value, color, or atmospheric shift;
- separation or collapse of foreground, middle ground, and background;
- whether objects sit, float, stack, interlock, or behave like collage pieces;
- whether spatial contradictions are accidental or systematic;
- how paths, architecture, figures, or repeated forms direct depth;
- how the space reinforces flatness, realism, theatricality, surrealism, or another visible condition.

Do not write only “no perspective.” Explain what replaces conventional perspective.

## 5. Shape and form language

Analyze:

- organic versus geometric construction;
- round, angular, inflated, compressed, elongated, fragmented, regular, or irregular silhouettes;
- degree of simplification, stylization, symbolization, distortion, or realism;
- whether recognition depends mainly on silhouette, color block, outline, or internal structure;
- consistency of abstraction across different objects;
- repeated shape families and meaningful contrasts;
- how shape construction affects character, weight, rhythm, and visual unity.

## 6. Line system

Analyze:

- presence and role of contour lines;
- line color, value, opacity, thickness, and continuity;
- uniformity or variation in line weight;
- smoothness, mechanical precision, wobble, pressure change, breaks, or open contours;
- relationship between outer contour and internal structure lines;
- whether internal lines describe structure, pattern, material, volume, movement, or decoration;
- consistency of line treatment across image regions;
- contribution of line to precision, softness, hand-made quality, graphic flatness, or density.

## 7. Color and value system

Analyze:

- dominant, supporting, and accent color families;
- warm/cool distribution by area, not only by color list;
- saturation and lightness ranges;
- location and function of the darkest and lightest masses;
- whether object colors follow local color or a transformed palette;
- background-to-subject color relationships;
- whether adjacent forms separate through hue, value, temperature, outline, or texture;
- concentration or dispersal of accents;
- presence of global tinting, mutedness, fluorescence, chalkiness, transparency, or opacity, supported by visible evidence;
- how color distribution creates hierarchy, rhythm, balance, atmosphere, or tension.

Use measured color values only when they materially clarify the analysis. Treat sampled values as approximate if texture, compression, or lighting affects them.

## 8. Light and tonal construction

Do not reduce this dimension to “has light” or “has no light.” Analyze:

- whether a directional, diffuse, ambient, symbolic, or inconsistent light source is implied;
- whether light direction remains coherent across objects;
- presence and form of highlights, lit planes, halftones, core shadows, reflected light, ambient occlusion, and cast shadows;
- whether value changes describe volume, depth, material, atmosphere, or merely color variation;
- whether highlights are modeled transitions or separate flat graphic shapes;
- whether dark areas come from illumination, local color, outlines, or compositional contrast;
- how objects remain legible when conventional light modeling is weak or absent;
- overall value hierarchy and contrast range;
- how the light/value strategy strengthens flatness, volume, drama, softness, ambiguity, decoration, or another stylistic quality.

## 9. Surface texture and material treatment

Analyze:

- where texture appears: background, fills, lines, edges, specific objects, or the whole frame;
- grain size, density, contrast, direction, and spatial consistency;
- fine noise versus larger mottling, fibers, stains, brush marks, scratches, dots, or repeated digital patterns;
- whether texture follows shape boundaries or behaves like a global overlay;
- whether different colors carry different texture behavior;
- whether materials are differentiated or deliberately given the same surface language;
- visibility of texture at normal viewing size versus close inspection;
- whether texture supports the forms or competes with them;
- what production process the texture might evoke, while keeping actual process attribution uncertain unless independently known.

## 10. Detail density and abstraction hierarchy

Analyze:

- overall information density;
- where details are concentrated and where they are intentionally withheld;
- average amount and type of internal information used to identify ordinary objects;
- contrast between simple silhouettes and complex internal patterning;
- whether details serve recognition, material realism, decoration, narrative, or spatial description;
- consistency or hierarchy of abstraction among figures, objects, and environments;
- how detail density controls visual breathing, rhythm, focus, and perceived sophistication.

## 11. Repetition, rhythm, and visual movement

Analyze:

- repeated curves, stripes, circles, edges, motifs, colors, or directional gestures;
- variation in scale, spacing, orientation, and frequency;
- mechanical repetition versus irregular recurrence;
- connections among distant image regions through shared forms or colors;
- how repetition directs the eye and creates tempo;
- whether movement feels flowing, pulsing, static, scattered, or directional, and why.

## 12. Atmosphere and perceptual character

Ground every characterization in visible features:

- identify the image's perceptual character, such as quiet, playful, tense, absurd, ceremonial, dense, detached, or intimate;
- explain which composition, palette, form, space, line, light, or detail choices produce that effect;
- distinguish visible atmosphere from claims about author intention or audience emotion;
- avoid unsupported evaluative words such as “premium,” “healing,” “sophisticated,” or “retro.”

## Decisive style mechanisms

After completing the dimensions, identify 3–5 cross-dimensional mechanisms. A mechanism is stronger than a keyword: it explains how several visible choices cooperate.

For each mechanism, state:

1. the visible evidence;
2. the dimensions it connects;
3. the effect it produces;
4. why it is more decisive than secondary traits;
5. what materially changes if it is removed.
