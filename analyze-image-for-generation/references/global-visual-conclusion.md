# Global Visual Conclusion

## Contents

1. Graphical modeling and object-modeling system
2. Visual layering and visibility
3. Color, fill, shape, volume, and detail
4. Density rhythm, visual organization, and spatial construction
5. Writing rules

## Purpose

Write a compact but information-dense conclusion about the image's visible construction. Omit sampling history and reasoning narration; retain degree, scope, and decisive exceptions.

Do not substitute style labels for mechanisms. If a label is useful, place it only after describing the visible construction.

## 1. Graphical modeling

Describe these as independent axes:

- **Planarity:** fully flat, predominantly flat, flat with faceted volume, or continuously modeled.
- **Construction precision:** mechanically regular, geometry-led with small deviations, visibly hand-shaped, or freely irregular.
- **Representation degree:** realistic, simplified realistic, highly summarized, icon-like, symbolic, or near-abstract.
- **Recognition mechanism:** silhouette, outline, color block, internal structure, tonal volume, material detail, text, or symbols.
- **Interior modeling:** absent, sparse structural divisions, discrete light/mid/dark planes, continuous gradients, or complex material modeling.
- **Deformation:** strict proportion, controlled exaggeration, inflation, compression, skew, inconsistent perspective, or mixed abstraction.

Do not collapse “flat” and “vector-like” into one judgment. Flatness describes depth modeling; vector precision describes edge regularity and construction.

## 2. Object-modeling system

Conclude:

- dominant modeling mode and named local exceptions;
- recognition strategy and shape grammar;
- primary and secondary mass hierarchy, attachments, and protected clean masses;
- value-plane count, light-to-dark order, value span, adjacent-plane contrast, and edge behavior;
- gradient policy and lighting ceiling for highlights, shadows, cast shadows, ambient occlusion, specular response, reflections, transparency, refraction, and glow;
- material-response classes and their allowed cues;
- line-based volume rules;
- object-detail density and progression across primary, supporting, background, and nested-scene tiers;
- over-modeling and under-modeling signs.

Follow `object-modeling.md`. Keep each intensity axis independent and state both required behavior and its upper limit.

## 3. Visual-layering system

Conclude:

- global spatial-order status and meaningful back-to-front bands;
- decisive local front/rear relations, overlap types, and interlocks;
- contour ownership, termination, reappearance, shared borders, and outline redraw;
- visibility profiles for primary, major, and compositionally important small front elements;
- masks, clipping, windows, cutouts, knockouts, and transparent overlays;
- production-stage evidence and whether exact authoring order is recoverable;
- transferable layer-role grammar, visibility priorities, and protected identity cues;
- layer-order failure signs.

Follow `layering-hidden-shapes.md`. Keep projected size, spatial depth, production order, and perceptual priority independent.

## 4. Color construction

Conclude:

- functional color roles and approximate area shares;
- saturation range and whether saturation is uniform or concentrated;
- lightness distribution and overall value span;
- darkest and lightest functions;
- hue breadth: monochrome, adjacent, limited, or multi-hue;
- hue-distance relations among the largest roles;
- warm/cool balance and spatial distribution;
- warm/cool/neutral area allocation and counterweight role;
- value ladder from lightest to darkest role;
- dominant area colors versus concentrated accents;
- opacity, transparency, mixing, and adjacency policies between colors;
- separation devices for every decisive neighboring pair;
- whether color follows local object color or a transformed palette.

Avoid vague phrases such as “colorful,” “bold palette,” or “high-saturation accents” unless the sentence also states where, how much, and how the colors interact.
Follow `color-relationships.md` for palette-mode selection, role mapping, recoloring, and adhesion checks.

## 5. Fill mechanism

Describe:

- flat fill, gradient, wash, modeled paint, or mixed behavior;
- dense or thin application;
- opaque or translucent;
- evenness, brush marks, gaps, bleeding, overprint, or registration offset;
- whether interiors contain light, texture, or material variation;
- whether fills obey contours or break their boundaries.

Keep color and fill separate: color describes chromatic organization; fill describes how color occupies shapes.

## 6. Shape and deformation

Describe:

- geometric versus organic construction;
- repeated primitives;
- angular, rounded, elongated, compressed, fragmented, or irregular silhouettes;
- consistency of abstraction across objects;
- whether shape derives from object geometry, lettering, brush movement, or decoration.

## 7. Volume and light

Describe:

- flatness, faceted planes, or continuous modeling;
- directional, diffuse, symbolic, or inconsistent light;
- highlights, side planes, shadows, cast shadows, and reflections;
- whether light is physically coherent or used as graphic separation;
- what keeps objects legible when conventional shading is absent.

## 8. Detail distribution

Describe:

- dense and quiet zones;
- internal detail level of ordinary objects;
- whether detail serves recognition, structure, text, decoration, material, or narrative;
- whether the focal area is denser than the rest of the frame;
- how identity, structural, decorative, and surface microdetails are distributed within representative objects;
- which object tiers preserve clean interiors and which may receive denser modeling.

Do not say “no detail” when the actual condition is “flat color interiors with detail concentrated in text, hardware, or decoration.”
Keep object-detail density separate from canvas-level density rhythm.

## 9. Density rhythm and negative space

Conclude:

- quiet, transition, medium, and dense regions;
- approximate area dominance and continuity of the largest quiet field;
- number and separation of density peaks;
- direction and abruptness of density transitions;
- breathing gaps between information clusters;
- clean versus decorated portions of the primary subject;
- which edges, contrasts, text, overlaps, or textures create each dense region.

Keep this independent from object count and detail hierarchy. Follow `density-rhythm.md` for the full method.

## 10. Visual organization

Conclude:

- first-read entry, focal hierarchy, and viewing path;
- dominant visual mass, counterweights, balance axis, and edge tension;
- repeated motif families and their cadence or mutation;
- graphic devices that present, connect, or sequence information;
- semantic groups, their hierarchy, and their connectors.

Follow `visual-organization.md`. Keep visual weight distinct from geometric center and density, and keep semantic groups distinct from production layers.

## 11. Spatial construction

Describe:

- horizon and perspective presence;
- overlap, scale change, foreshortening, visible side planes, and vertical placement;
- flattened, staged, stacked, interlocking, collage-like, or volumetric space;
- whether one physical camera can be recovered.

## Writing rules

- Write one compact paragraph per dimension when the user requests a full blueprint.
- State the conclusion directly; do not list the crops or comparisons used to reach it.
- Use “global,” “dominant,” “local,” or “mixed” when scope matters.
- State important exceptions in the same paragraph.
- Prefer concrete visual mechanisms over mood, movement, period, medium, or genre labels.
